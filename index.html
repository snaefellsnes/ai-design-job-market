Before you scroll further

On "AI slop," commodified design, and why the designer's eye still matters

If it stings to hear "just have AI do it," trust that reaction. Outsiders underestimate how much judgment a craft takes — and AI makes "close enough" look more available than it is. Worse, easy-to-produce, competent-looking output ("AI slop") can give non-designers real confidence they now understand design, pushing the market to price it like a commodity instead of a craft.

The fix isn't denying AI can do parts of the job — sometimes it can, and that's worth taking seriously. It's naming, out loud, the part it still can't: the psychology of how people actually behave and make mistakes at an interface. When a room isn't convinced, ask real questions instead of overriding the decision — what's the reasoning here? will users actually use this metric? does it hold up at scale? A good answer teaches you something; no answer makes the gap obvious on its own.

Learn just enough code to understand what engineers actually do

You don't need to become an engineer — but a little real fluency in how code works changes every conversation with the people who build what you design. You'll ask sharper questions in standup, scope feasibility yourself instead of guessing, spot when a "quick fix" isn't actually quick, and be taken more seriously in technical rooms — which matters most early on, when you're still building that credibility.

The goal: enough working knowledge of how a webpage or app is actually built that you can read a pull request description or a bug report and follow what's going on.

Position yourself as a leader, no matter your level

Leadership isn't gated behind a manager title — and if you're early in your career, you're the person this applies to most. Reposition for the job you want

Once you're comfortable with AI, this is where it turns into an actual job search — pulling roles you genuinely want, finding what they have in common, and rebuilding your LinkedIn, resume, and portfolio around that pattern instead of a generic version of your career.

The goal: a LinkedIn, resume, and portfolio that all tell the same, specific story about the kind of designer you are.

It's all about framing. The facts of your career don't need to change — how you frame them does. The same experience can read as "did a bunch of stuff" or as "solved this specific kind of problem, repeatedly, with this kind of impact." Whichever framing you land on in Step 2, it needs to show up identically on your LinkedIn, your resume, and your portfolio — a hiring manager who looks at all three should feel like they're reading about one person with one clear story, not three loosely-related documents.

1. Pull 5 job descriptions you genuinely love

Not five you're settling for — five that, if the offer landed tomorrow, you'd actually say yes to. This is the raw material for everything that follows, so it's worth being picky here.

The goal: a folder with the full text of 5 real postings, saved somewhere permanent — postings get taken down, so copy the whole thing, not just the link.

2. Run them through Claude or ChatGPT

Paste all five in and ask what they have in common: the problem the team is actually trying to solve, the industry or domain pattern, and — the part that matters most — what's implied but never said outright. Then ask how to frame your own background so those unspoken things are obvious at a glance.

The goal: a short, plain-language summary of the pattern across all 5 roles, and 3–5 phrases or framings you can reuse across your LinkedIn, resume, and portfolio.

A prompt to start with

Here are 5 job descriptions for roles I'd genuinely love to have. [paste all 5, full text] Look across all of them and tell me: 1. What do they have in common — responsibilities, tools, and phrasing that repeat? 2. What underlying problem is each team actually trying to solve by hiring for this role? 3. What industry or domain pattern connects them, if any? 4. What's implied but not stated outright — the "hidden" requirement or ideal candidate trait each one is really looking for? 5. Given all of that, how should I frame my own background so the things they're looking for are obvious within the first few seconds of reading about me?

3. Update your LinkedIn

Same positioning from Step 2, applied everywhere someone will actually look.

The goal: a profile where the header, photo, About section, and job entries all say the same thing about you.

  1. AHeader/banner — a simple, current visual that matches how you're now positioning yourself.
  2. BProfile picture — recent, professional, and warm; this is a trust signal before anyone reads a word.
  3. CAbout section — open with the framing from Step 2, not a list of job titles.
  4. DJob entries — rewrite bullet points to echo the language the job descriptions used, where it's honestly true of your work.

4. Rebuild your resume around it

Same framing as your LinkedIn — a hiring manager who looks at both should see one consistent story, not two different people.

The goal: a resume where the top third makes your fit for these 5 roles obvious, even to someone skimming for six seconds.

A good starting point for your bullets: Google's XYZ formula — "Accomplished [X], as measured by [Y], by doing [Z]." Doesn't need to apply to every line, but it's a solid default: X is the outcome you drove (not the task you were handed), Y is the number that proves it, Z is specifically how you got there. Example: "Redesigned the onboarding flow (X), cutting first-week drop-off 22% (Y) by collapsing six steps into three (Z)." Read Laszlo Bock's original formula →

5. Restructure your portfolio case studies

Every case study should answer two questions, in order: what was the opportunity or gap you were handed, and what was the actual impact of what you solved. Everything else in the case study is in service of those two things.

The goal: a portfolio where someone can understand your judgment and your impact even if they skim — not one that requires them to read every screen to "get it."
